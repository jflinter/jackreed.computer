Before you have finished driving out of the car rental place in the Anchorage airport, the Chugach mountains explode across your windshield. They casually abut the city, and their highest peaks dwarf any mountain east of the Rockies, but the rest of Alaska starts to overwhelm you so quickly that you start to forget they're there. Alaska has the kinds of Bigness and Wildness that you forget exist after spending too much time in the city. Mountains and glaciers and wildlife are goddamn everywhere. Knowing that it might be our last chance to properly visit a Big and Hard place like this for a while, Avery and I spent two weeks there in August 2026 for our babymoon.

We flew from Seattle to Anchorage, where we rented a car, went to REI, and then drove to Seward, AK. We didn’t know what to expect - Seward has 2600 year-round residents - but it gets a lot of tourism in the summer (like everywhere we went) and so has a place to get a decent taco, etc.

The glaciers grind up lots of silt, which is rich in nutrients, which goes into the water and lets lots of plankton etc grow, which in turn supports a ton of marine life.

Kenai Fjords NP only has one hiking trail - the Harding Ice Field hike - which we did the next day. You march up a steep and somewhat miserable trail for 4 hours, gaining increasingly dramatic views of the Exit Glacier, until finally the entire ice field which ultimately feeds all of the glaciers in the park opens up.

We took a water taxi across the bay into Kachemak Bay State Park (only accessible by boat and seaplane) and hiked to a lake at the base of Grewingk glacier to camp for a night.

The next day we hired a pilot to take us on a small plane to look at bears in Lake Clark National Park.

<RouteMap id="homer-lake-clark" />

<Figure id="318" />

<Figure id="382" />

Perhaps the vibiest moment of the trip - flying into the park, truly the middle of nowhere, huge mountains everywhere, asking the pilot where we're going to go. He says, "we'll look on the beach for bears, and when we see some, we'll land and go check them out!" Sounds good bro 🤙

We learned that while these are genetically the same thing as grizzly bears, scientists call them coastal brown bears. Bears in different habitats behave wildly differently, to the point that you would think they were different kinds of animal. In Lake Clark, bears live in utopia, feasting on ample salmon all summer. This means they mostly don't give a shit about people and you can walk right up to them. (Unlike, say, grizzly bears in Denali, which as we would later learn behave very differently.) Suffice to say we were shocked at the quantity of bears we encountered and how close our guide encouraged us to go to them.

Next we drove back through Anchorage for a quick refuel and then the second week of the trip, which we spent in and around Denali National Park.

<RouteMap id="homer-denali" />

Denali NP is wild. The park itself is the size of Vermont. The mountain and its mountain neighbors are in the middle, but given their isolation and the weather only 30% of park visitors actually get to see it. We didn't want to take any chances so we naturally booked our second bush plane of the trip, this time equipped with skis instead of fat tires, to get a closer look.

<Figure id="505" />

Human brains are not wired to experience this kind of scale. You have the sensation of literally not believing your eyes as you move across this landscape of giant mountains, like what you're seeing cannot be real.

Next we set about exploring the park by land. The national park itself has a delicate balancing act in that they get hundreds of thousands of mouth-breathing cruise ship tourists a year but want to preserve the wilderness feeling as much as possible. So there's one road through the middle of the park that's only open to the park's bus system. Beyond that the park has no facilities, no roads, and no trails.

If you want to camp, you go to the ranger station, they give you a long briefing focused disproportionately on bear safety, help you plan a route, then assign you a 50-square-mile section to camp in each night. This all but guarantees you won't see other people. You then take the bus into the park and just... get off and start walking into the underbrush.

<Figure id="537" />

We spent three nights hiking and camping in the park like this.

<RouteMap id="denali-hike" />

Probably the three most magical days of wilderness travel of our adult lives. We bushwhacked through thick underbrush, crossed rivers, hiked on alpine tundra (like a tempurpedic made out of moss), saw herds of caribou, and connected with each other, our surroundings, and our baby-to-be. We saw zero human beings, zero trails, zero signs of humanity save for the occasional airplane, and 3 grizzly bears.
